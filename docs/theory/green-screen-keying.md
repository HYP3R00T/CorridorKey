# Green Screen Keying

## What keying is

Keying is the process of separating a foreground subject from a background by colour. In green screen work, the subject is filmed in front of a uniformly lit green backdrop. A keyer analyses each pixel and decides whether it belongs to the subject (foreground) or the backdrop (background), producing an alpha matte that describes the boundary.

The result is a foreground layer with a transparent background that can be composited over any replacement background.

## Why chroma math is not enough

Traditional chroma keyers work by measuring how green a pixel is relative to its red and blue channels. Pixels above a threshold are declared background; pixels below are declared foreground; pixels in between are partially transparent.

This works well for clean, evenly lit green screens with hard-edged subjects. It breaks down on:

- Fine detail — hair, fur, translucent fabric
- Motion blur at subject edges
- Uneven green screen lighting (hot spots, shadows)
- Green reflections on the subject (spill)
- Subjects that contain green (clothing, props)

These cases require the keyer to understand the scene, not just measure colour ratios.

## What the neural network does

CorridorKey uses a transformer-based model trained on a large dataset of composited footage with ground-truth alpha mattes. The model takes a frame and its alpha hint as input and predicts a high-quality alpha matte.

Because the model has learned from many examples of real subjects against real green screens, it can handle fine hair, motion blur, and partial transparency in ways that chroma math cannot.

The alpha hint is a coarse matte — typically generated by a fast chroma keyer or a rotoscoping tool — that tells the model roughly where the subject is. The model uses this as a guide and refines it into a production-quality matte.

## The CNN refiner

After the transformer produces an initial matte, a CNN refiner corrects macroblocking artifacts at subject edges. These artifacts appear because transformers process the image in fixed-size patches, which can produce visible block boundaries at fine edges.

The refiner runs on the full frame (or in tiles on low-VRAM hardware) and produces the final sharp matte. Disabling the refiner is faster but results in visibly coarser edges.
